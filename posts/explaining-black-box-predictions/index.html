Explaining Black-Box Predictions: SHAP and What Comes After

A tour of SHAP and its alternatives — and the two problems that make black-box explanation genuinely hard: scale and multicollinearity.

interpretability
survey
Published

October 13, 2026

Introduction

Most models that end up in production raise two questions. Why did the model make this particular prediction? Which features actually matter, and which just add noise? Stakeholders want to know whether the features behind a prediction match their understanding of the domain. The team building the model needs to know which features earn their place, both to guide feature engineering and to debug unexpected behaviour.

For some models, the answer comes built-in. With normalised features and some regularisation (or a Bayesian prior), a coefficient in a linear model roughly tells you by how much its feature moves the prediction. High-performance non-linear models are by far less transparent. Boosted trees provide importance scores, but these are heuristic summaries with well-known biases, while neural networks have no built-in equivalent at all.

A useful explanation needs more than just ranking. It should point at the direction a feature moves the prediction: “low income raised the risk” is an explanation, while “income matters” isn’t. It should measure every contribution on the scale of the prediction itself, so you can compare one feature against another, or one model against the next. And it should be model-agnostic, treating the model as a black box, so the same method works for tree ensembles and neural networks alike.

Model-agnostic methods meet these requirements to varying degree. Permutation importance is simple and cheap, yet it only gives a global ranking, with no direction and no explanation of individual predictions. LIME explains individual predictions with signed coefficients, but these are only local approximations and don’t add up to the prediction. Shapley values meet every requirement and add guarantees on top: contributions sum exactly to the gap between the prediction and the average prediction, and they are consistent, so a feature’s Shapley value never decreases when its contribution to the model increases. That rigour comes at a price, and it’s where the real challenges begin.

The first challenge is scale. Computing a Shapley value means evaluating the model on every possible combination of features, and the number of combinations grows exponentially. Past a few dozen features, exact values are intractable. Sampling-based approximations help, but a single explanation can still take thousands of model calls.

The second challenge is correlation. When several features carry the same signal, the credit for that signal is split between them, sometimes evenly, sometimes arbitrarily. Each feature then looks weaker than the signal it represents, and small changes to the data or the model can reshuffle the split.

Correlation causes a subtler problem too: it distorts the estimate itself. Shapley values require evaluating the model with some features left out, but most models can’t accept missing inputs. In practice, a “removed” feature is replaced with values sampled from its marginal distribution, independently of the features that remain. This produces inputs that are unrealistic or outright impossible: dozens of transactions with almost no money spent, or more transactions in the past hour than in the past day. The explanation then rests on an average over data that could never occur.

Neither problem has a free fix, but both have good partial answers. The rest of this post looks at what they are, and what each one costs.

Explainability Landscape

Explainability methods fall into 3 main families: surrogate methods, removal-based methods, and model-specific methods (Covert et al. 2021). This post covers the first two — model-agnostic removal-based methods and model surrogates.

Surrogate Methods

Surrogate methods replace the black box with an interpretable model that imitates it, and read feature importance from that model instead. The resulting importance is only as reliable as the imitation. That imitation can target two different scopes: a global surrogate has to imitate the model everywhere, a local surrogate only has to imitate it around one prediction.

  • Decision tree. A shallow tree reproduces the model’s predictions with just a few splits. Greedy deep trees are unstable, since small changes in the data can change the top splits, so stable surrogates need globally optimised shallow trees, such as evolutionary trees (Grubinger et al. 2014) or optimal sparse trees (Lin et al. 2020).
  • Rule list. An ordered set of if-then rules, learned for example with RuleFit (Friedman and Popescu 2008) or Bayesian Rule Lists (Letham et al. 2015). Importance follows from which features appear in the rules and how much of the data each rule covers.
  • Additive models. The prediction is modelled as a sum of separate curves, one per feature, sometimes with a few pairwise interactions, as in Explainable Boosting Machines (Lou et al. 2013). Each curve shows how its feature moves the prediction, and the curve’s average absolute value across the data measures how much the feature matters.
  • Local surrogate. Instead of covering the whole input space, a local surrogate only needs to hold near one instance. Anchors (Ribeiro et al. 2018) searches for a small set of conditions on a few features — an if-then rule — under which the model’s prediction stays the same for almost any value of the other features, found by perturbing the input and checking how often the rule still holds across the perturbations. The output isn’t a magnitude or a direction like the surrogates above; it’s just which conditions hold and how reliably they pin the prediction down.

Surrogates are appealing, but they often struggle to keep up with high-performing models. With hundreds of features, a shallow tree or a short rule list can use only a handful of them. A plain GAM scales to many features but has no interactions; an EBM adds pairwise ones, but only a small fraction of the possible pairs, and every pair it adds makes the surrogate harder to read. The surrogate can then fit the model poorly, and its importances describe the surrogate rather than the model. A local surrogate like Anchors sidesteps that scaling problem entirely, since it only ever has to explain one neighbourhood — at the cost of giving up any global view of the model.

Removal-Based Methods

Instead of imitating the model from the outside, removal-based methods probe it directly: they simulate removing features, typically by replacing them with background values, and measure the effect. They differ in which effect they measure: model’s global error, or a single prediction.

Change in Error Methods

These methods need true labels and give only global scores with no direction.

  • Permutation-based. Permutation importance (Breiman 2001) shuffles one feature’s column and measures how much the error grows. If two features interact, shuffling either one destroys the whole interaction, so each gets full credit for it. With correlated features, it also creates unrealistic rows, extrapolating the model into regions of the input space it never saw in training (Hooker et al. 2021).
    • Conditional permutation importance (Strobl et al. 2008) avoids this by shuffling a feature only among rows with similar values of its correlated features
    • Grouped permutation importance (Gregorutti et al. 2015) shuffles correlated features together, which keeps rows realistic but gives one score for the whole group
  • Retraining-based. Drop-column importance retrains the model without a feature and measures how much the error grows; LOCO (Lei et al. 2018) adds confidence intervals to the same idea. Because a new model is trained each time, correlated features can stand in for each other: dropping either one costs little, so both features score near zero even if the original model relied on them. This is by far the most computationally costly approach.
  • Shapley-based. SAGE (Covert et al. 2020) applies Shapley values to the model’s error, so the feature scores add up to the model’s total error reduction. An interaction’s effect is split between the features involved instead of being counted in full for each.

In practice, drop-column importance and LOCO are too expensive for models with hundreds of features, which leaves permutation importance and SAGE. The difference between them is interactions. When the model combines two features, say approving a loan only if income is high and debt is low, shuffling either one breaks the combination, so permutation importance gives each feature the full effect and counts it twice. SAGE averages each feature’s effect over all subsets of the others, so the effect is split between the features and the scores add up to the model’s actual performance.

Change in a Single Prediction Methods

The other branch of removal-based methods leaves the model’s overall error aside and asks how one prediction changes without a feature.

  • Local approximation. LIME (Ribeiro et al. 2016) evaluates the model on many perturbed copies of one input, each resampling every feature independently from the training data and so ignoring correlations, then fits a linear model weighted by closeness to the input. Its coefficients are an averaged local slope: signed sensitivities that shift with the neighbourhood size and the samples, and don’t sum to the prediction.
  • Shapley values have a fixed target with guarantees: contributions sum to the prediction minus the average prediction, and a feature’s value never drops when its contribution grows. Exact computation is intractable; the estimators below target the same values and differ in where the cost goes.
    • Averaging. Štrumbelj & Kononenko (Štrumbelj and Kononenko 2010, 2014) sample combinations of the other features and average the prediction difference with and without the feature, drawing off features from their marginals. The estimate is exact in the limit, but every explanation needs fresh model calls.
    • Regression. KernelSHAP (Lundberg and Lee 2017) fits one weighted regression on sampled combinations whose coefficients are the Shapley values, exactly so with all combinations. Each call informs every feature, so it usually needs fewer calls.
    • Amortised. FastSHAP (Jethani et al. 2022) trains a network once, with KernelSHAP’s objective, to output all Shapley values for any input. Each explanation becomes one forward pass; accuracy depends on training.
    • Beyond marginal sampling. Marginal draws create unrealistic inputs. Two alternatives:
      • Conditional. Off features are drawn given the on ones, for example via a Gaussian copula (Aas et al. 2021). Inputs stay realistic, but credit follows correlations: a feature the model ignores can take part of a correlated feature’s credit, where marginal sampling gives it zero. It is also costlier.
      • Grouped. A cluster of correlated features is switched on or off together (Jullum et al. 2021). Inputs stay realistic and credit isn’t split arbitrarily, but you get one value per group.

Compared with LIME, Shapley values answer a fixed question: estimators converge to the same numbers, while LIME’s coefficients depend on its neighbourhood and samples. Contributions sum to the prediction, so they compare across features and average into global importances, and interactions are split by a principled rule. The cost is computation. On correlated features, both share the weakness of marginal sampling, which conditional or grouped sampling addresses for Shapley values.

Model-Specific Methods

Model-specific methods read importance from a model’s internals instead of probing it from the outside.

  • Embedded. Gain in tree ensembles comes free as a by-product of training. But it is computed on the training data, so it rewards overfitting; it favours features with many distinct values, gives no direction, and can drop when the model relies on a feature more (Strobl et al. 2007).
  • Gradient- and propagation-based. These methods work backward through a neural network. Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al. 2017) averages gradients along a straight line from a reference input to the actual input, since a single gradient is zero wherever the model is flat; the scores sum to the change in prediction. LRP (Bach et al. 2015) passes the output score itself backward, splitting it among each layer’s inputs by their contribution, so the scores sum to the prediction.
  • Model-specific SHAP.
    • TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al. 2018) computes Shapley values exactly by walking the trees: at splits on features that are on, it follows the input’s branch; at splits on features that are off, it follows both branches. With background data, it gives exact marginal values; without it, it uses the training counts stored in the tree as a rough stand-in for conditional sampling. Grouping isn’t built in.
    • DeepSHAP (Shrikumar et al. 2017) and GradientSHAP (Erion et al. 2021) approximate Shapley values by running DeepLIFT or Integrated Gradients against many background samples instead of one reference, and averaging.

These methods trade generality for speed and, for TreeSHAP, freedom from sampling error. They don’t escape the correlation problem: most fill off features from background samples, as marginal sampling does, path-dependent TreeSHAP only roughly approximates conditional sampling, and none supports grouping natively. Each also works only for its model family.

Shapley Values, and why exact SHAP doesn’t scale

Of the methods surveyed above, Shapley values are the one every other removal-based and model-specific approach either implements, approximates, or gets compared against — so it’s worth seeing exactly how they’re defined. SHAP (Lundberg and Lee 2017) brings the Shapley value from cooperative game theory (Shapley 1953) to feature attribution, and shows that earlier methods such as LIME belong to the same family of additive attributions. Figure 5 breaks the formula’s weight down into three steps, using a toy model with \(|F|=4\): feature \(i\) plus 3 others.

Step 1 — count. Group the \(2^3 = 8\) combinations of the other 3 features by size \(k = |S|\). There are \(\binom{3}{k}\) combinations of each size: \(\binom{3}{0}=1\), \(\binom{3}{1}=3\), \(\binom{3}{2}=3\), \(\binom{3}{3}=1\) — e.g. 3 ways to pick 2 of the 3 features, which is why the diagram labels those bands “3 combinations, same weight.”

Step 2 — weight. The total weight splits equally across the \(|F|=4\) possible sizes, then equally again across the \(\binom{|F|-1}{k}\) combinations that share a size: \(\frac{1}{|F|}\cdot\frac{1}{\binom{|F|-1}{k}} = \frac{k!\,(|F|-k-1)!}{|F|!}\). That’s why the lone size-0 and size-3 combinations each keep the whole \(1/4\), while each of the 3 size-1 and each of the 3 size-2 combinations gets \(1/12\) — shown directly next to each row.

Step 3 — mean effect. Each combination’s weight multiplies its marginal contribution \(f_{S \cup \{i\}}(x_{S \cup \{i\}}) - f_S(x_S)\) — the diagram’s right column, how much the prediction changes when \(i\) is added to exactly that combination. The weights sum to 1, so this is a weighted mean: sum all 8 weighted terms and the result is \(\varphi_i\), feature \(i\)’s average effect across every combination it could be added to. For an input \(x\), that whole picture is the formula

\[ \varphi_i(x) = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|F| - |S| - 1)!}{|F|!} \big[ f_{S \cup \{i\}}(x_{S \cup \{i\}}) - f_S(x_S) \big] \]

Notation. \(x\) is the input being explained; \(F\) is the set of all features and \(|F|\) their number; \(S\) is a combination of the other features; \(x_S\) are the input’s values for the features in \(S\); \(f_S\) is a model that uses only the features in \(S\).

Because every combination is considered, an interaction’s effect is shared between the features involved instead of being credited in full to each, as permutation importance does.

In the original formulation, \(f_S\) is a separate model retrained on just the features in \(S\). Exact computation therefore has two costs that multiply: each feature has \(2^{|F|-1}\) combinations, and each combination needs its own retrained model. With hundreds of features, this is computationally intractable.

SHAP approximates these values without retraining. Instead of training a model without the features outside \(S\), it keeps the one trained model \(f\) and integrates those features out, averaging the model’s prediction over the values they could take. The integral has no closed form for a black-box model, so it is estimated numerically, by averaging over sampled values:

\[ f_S(x_S) = \int f\big(x_S,\, x_{\bar S}\big)\, q\big(x_{\bar S}\big)\, dx_{\bar S} \approx \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} f\big(x_S,\, \tilde x^{(j)}_{\bar S}\big), \qquad \tilde x^{(j)}_{\bar S} \sim q\big(x_{\bar S}\big) \]

\[ q\big(x_{\bar S}\big) = \begin{cases} p\big(x_{\bar S}\big) & \text{marginal (classic approaches)} \\[4pt] p\big(x_{\bar S} \mid x_S\big) & \text{conditional} \end{cases} \]

Notation. \(f\) is the trained model; \(\bar S\) are the features not in \(S\); \(q(x_{\bar S})\) is the distribution their values are drawn from; \(p(x_{\bar S})\) is their marginal distribution, and \(p(x_{\bar S} \mid x_S)\) their distribution given the known features; \(\tilde x^{(1)}_{\bar S}, \dots, \tilde x^{(m)}_{\bar S}\) are \(m\) sampled values for those features.

Classic approaches use the marginal distribution, taking the missing values from random background rows of the data. Conditional versions draw them given the known features, so that the sampled values stay consistent with the input. The difference matters when features are correlated, as discussed below.

With all features known, the model gives its actual prediction; with none, the integral becomes the average prediction over the data. The SHAP values sum to the gap between the two:

\[ \sum_{i \in F} \varphi_i(x) = f(x) - \mathbb{E}[f(X)] \]

Notation. \(f(x) = f_F(x)\) is the actual prediction for \(x\); \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = f_\varnothing\) is the model’s average prediction over the data.

SAGE (Covert et al. 2020) applies the same weighting to the model’s performance over the whole dataset instead of to a single prediction. In place of the prediction, it uses the gain: how much lower the model’s loss is when it knows the features in \(S\) than when it just predicts the average:

\[ G(S) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\ell\big(f_\varnothing, Y\big)\big]}_{\text{loss of predicting the average}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\ell\big(f_S(X_S), Y\big)\big]}_{\text{loss using the features in } S} \]

Notation. \(Y\) is the true label; \(\ell\) is the loss function, e.g. squared error or cross-entropy; the expectations run over the data; \(f_\varnothing\) and \(f_S\) are as defined above, with the missing features integrated out in the same way.

The gain grows as the loss falls: it is zero with no features and largest when the features help the most. The SAGE value of feature \(i\) is

\[ \varphi_i^{\text{SAGE}} = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|F| - |S| - 1)!}{|F|!} \big[ G(S \cup \{i\}) - G(S) \big] \]

Notation. \(G(S)\) is the gain defined above; \(F\), \(S\) and the weights are the same as in the SHAP formula.

The difference \(G(S \cup \{i\}) - G(S)\) is the extra gain from adding feature \(i\) to the combination \(S\), so a feature that helps gets a positive value. Since the gain is a drop in loss, “gain with \(i\) minus gain without \(i\)” is the same as “loss without \(i\) minus loss with \(i\)”. Interactions are shared in the same way as in SHAP. Because SAGE measures performance, it requires true labels. Its values sum to the total gain: on a dataset of \(n\) rows, the loss of predicting the average minus the loss of the model. Each value therefore reads as a feature’s share of the model’s improvement over always predicting the average:

\[ \sum_{i \in F} \varphi_i^{\text{SAGE}} = G(F) - G(\varnothing) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} \ell\big(\bar f,\, y_k\big)}_{\text{loss of predicting the average}} \; - \; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} \ell\big(f(x_k),\, y_k\big)}_{\text{loss of the model}} \]

Notation. \(G(F)\) is the gain with all features and \(G(\varnothing) = 0\) the gain with none; \((x_k, y_k)\) for \(k = 1, \dots, n\) are the rows of the dataset and their labels; \(\bar f = \frac{1}{n}\sum_k f(x_k)\) is the average prediction.

Integrating features out removes the retraining, but not the \(2^{|F|-1}\) combinations, each of which now costs \(m\) model calls. Sampling estimators evaluate only a random subset of the combinations rather than all of them (Štrumbelj and Kononenko 2014). That makes SHAP tractable, though each explanation still takes many model calls. KernelSHAP reduces the count by fitting one regression to all sampled combinations, estimating every feature’s value at once. For tree ensembles, TreeSHAP removes sampling altogether, computing exact values quickly by traversing the trees (Lundberg et al. 2018).

SAGE has a cheaper estimator of its own. Because the gain averages over rows, a SAGE value is an average over rows of each row’s average over combinations. Since combinations are drawn independently of rows, the two averages collapse into one over (row, combination) pairs:

\[ \varphi_i^{\text{SAGE}} = \mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)}\Big[\mathbb{E}_S\big[\Delta_i(X,Y,S)\big]\Big] = \mathbb{E}_{(X,Y,S)}\big[\Delta_i(X,Y,S)\big] \]

where

\[ \Delta_i(x, y, S) = \underbrace{\ell\big(f_S(x_S),\, y\big)}_{\text{loss without } i} \;-\; \underbrace{\ell\big(f_{S \cup \{i\}}(x_{S \cup \{i\}}),\, y\big)}_{\text{loss with } i} \]

Notation. \(\Delta_i(x, y, S)\) is the drop in loss on one row when feature \(i\) is added to the combination \(S\); \(\mathbb{E}_S\) averages over combinations, drawn with the Shapley weights; \(\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)}\) averages over the rows of the dataset.

SAGE estimates this single expectation directly. Each step draws a random row and a random ordering of the features, adds the features one at a time, and records how much each one lowers the loss for that row. The SAGE values are the averages of these drops over all steps. Computing SHAP values of the loss for every row and then averaging would instead estimate each row’s inner expectation separately, multiplying the cost by the number of rows. Covert et al. (2020) report that the direct route needs two to four orders of magnitude fewer model calls.

The marginal choice is the default in KernelSHAP, SAGE, and TreeSHAP with background data, so with correlated features, the model is scored on unrealistic inputs. The remedies are conditional versions, which sample from \(p(x_{\bar S} \mid x_S)\), and grouped versions, which treat correlated features as one. Both are discussed below. TreeSHAP’s path-dependent mode, used without background data, is a rough conditional approximation of this kind.

The two answer different questions. SHAP explains individual predictions: it shows which way each feature pushed a prediction, needs no labels, and averages into a global view. SAGE explains what the model’s accuracy depends on: features that move predictions without improving them score low, which suits feature selection and tracing drops in performance, and it gets there far more cheaply than averaging SHAP.

Cutting the per-sample cost: FastSHAP

That still leaves the scale problem raised at the start unsolved: every estimator above, sampling-based or exact, pays its cost again for every new input. The most efficient class of methods sidesteps per-sample computation entirely by amortising it: train a parametric function once, up front, that maps any input directly to its Shapley values in a single forward pass. The current state of the art is FastSHAP (Jethani et al. 2022).

Figure 6 shows the shift: instead of running Monte Carlo sampling for each input, FastSHAP trains two models, an explainer that outputs the Shapley values and a surrogate that handles missing features.

The explainer. FastSHAP starts from an alternative characterisation of Shapley values (Charnes et al. 1988). For a fixed input, they are the coefficients of the additive model that best reproduces the predictions on every combination of features, weighted by the Shapley kernel:

\[ \varphi(x) = \arg\min_{\phi \in \mathbb{R}^{|F|}} \; \mathbb{E}_{S \sim p_{\text{Sh}}} \Big[ \big( f_S(x_S) - f_\varnothing - \textstyle\sum_{j \in S} \phi_j \big)^2 \Big] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \textstyle\sum_{j \in F} \phi_j = f(x) - f_\varnothing \]

\[ p_{\text{Sh}}(S) \propto \frac{|F| - 1}{\binom{|F|}{|S|}\, |S|\, \big(|F| - |S|\big)}, \qquad 0 < |S| < |F| \]

Notation. \(\phi\) is a candidate vector of Shapley values, one per feature; \(p_{\text{Sh}}\) is the Shapley kernel, the distribution over combinations \(S\) that makes the solution equal the Shapley values; \(f_S\), \(f_\varnothing\) and \(F\) are as in the previous section.

KernelSHAP solves this problem separately for every input, from sampled combinations. FastSHAP instead trains one network \(\phi_\theta(x)\) to solve it for all inputs at once, by minimising the same objective averaged over the data:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{X}\, \mathbb{E}_{S \sim p_{\text{Sh}}} \Big[ \big( f_S(X_S) - f_\varnothing - \textstyle\sum_{j \in S} \phi_\theta(X)_j \big)^2 \Big] \]

Notation. \(\phi_\theta(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{|F|}\) is the explainer network’s output for input \(x\), with weights \(\theta\); \(\phi_\theta(x)_j\) is its estimate for feature \(j\); \(X\) is an input drawn from the training data.

No ground-truth Shapley values are needed: each training step samples an input and a combination, and the error of the additive fit is the training signal. If the network is expressive enough, the data large enough and the optimisation perfect, the minimiser outputs the exact Shapley values for every input.

The surrogate. Each evaluation of \(f_S\) in the loss would normally average the model over \(m\) background samples, the expensive part of classic SHAP. FastSHAP replaces this average with a second network \(g_\beta\), which sees an input with the features outside \(S\) masked out and is trained to reproduce the model’s prediction on the full input:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\beta) = \mathbb{E}_{X}\, \mathbb{E}_{S} \Big[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big( f(X) \,\big\|\, g_\beta(\mathrm{mask}(X, S)) \big) \Big] \]

Notation. \(g_\beta\) is the surrogate network, with weights \(\beta\); \(\mathrm{mask}(x, S)\) replaces the features outside \(S\) with a mask token; \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) is the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the model’s and the surrogate’s predicted class probabilities.

Minimising this KL divergence drives the surrogate to output the model’s prediction averaged over the missing features, given the visible ones (Covert et al. 2021):

\[ g_{\beta^*}\big(\mathrm{mask}(x, S)\big) = \int f\big(x_S,\, x_{\bar S}\big)\, p\big(x_{\bar S} \mid x_S\big)\, dx_{\bar S} = f_S(x_S) \]

This is the conditional choice of \(q\) from the previous section, learned instead of sampled. Each evaluation of \(f_S\) now costs one forward pass instead of \(m\), and the resulting explanations reflect the information each feature carries rather than how the model’s formula uses it.

The catch. One set of weights \(\theta\) produces the values for every input and every feature, and it is trained to minimise the error averaged over the data. The accuracy of any single explanation therefore depends on how well the network generalises, not on how much computation is spent on that input. A Monte Carlo estimate can always be tightened by drawing more samples for the input in question; a FastSHAP estimate cannot, and its error for a given feature on a given input is not bounded. The surrogate adds a second layer of approximation on top. In return, Jethani et al. (2022) report that FastSHAP’s single forward pass matches the accuracy that sampling estimators reach only after 200 to 2,000 model evaluations.

Paying for accuracy only where it’s needed: Selective Explanations

Selective Explanations (Paes et al. 2024) is a hybrid: use the fast amortized estimate where it’s already trustworthy, and fall back to the slow but accurate Monte Carlo estimate everywhere else.

\[ \text{SE}(x, y) \;\triangleq\; \begin{cases} \text{Amor}(x, y) & \text{if } \tau_\alpha(x) = 1, \\[4pt] \lambda(x)\,\text{Amor}(x, y) + \big(1 - \lambda(x)\big)\,\text{MC}^n(x, y) & \text{if } \tau_\alpha(x) = 0, \end{cases} \]

\(\tau_\alpha(x) = \mathbb{1}\{s(x) \le t_\alpha\}\) compares the input’s uncertainty score to a calibrated cutoff: 1 if the amortized estimate is trustworthy enough to use as-is, 0 otherwise. \(\lambda(x)\) is a blending weight fitted per band of uncertainty on a calibration set. Two ways of defining the uncertainty score \(s(x)\) are proposed:

Deep uncertainty — train several amortized networks with different random seeds and treat their disagreement as the uncertainty signal:

\[ s^{\text{Deep}}(x) \;\triangleq\; \frac{1}{dk}\sum_{i=1}^{d} \mathrm{Var}\Big(\text{Amor}^1(x)_i, \dots, \text{Amor}^k(x)_i\Big) \]

Learned uncertainty — instead of an ensemble, train a function to directly predict the expected gap between the amortized and Monte Carlo estimates:

\[ s^{\text{Learn}} \in \operatorname*{argmin}_{s \in \mathcal{F}} \sum_{(x,y) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}} \big| s(x) - \ell\big(\text{Amor}(x;y), \text{MC}^n(x;y)\big) \big|^2 \]

Selective Explanations doesn’t fix the multicollinearity problem discussed next: correlated features still get their joint contribution split unstably among themselves in either branch, since neither Amor nor the Monte Carlo estimator resolves that on its own. It also carries a subtler mismatch. Amor’s surrogate is trained to match the conditional value function, but the Monte Carlo estimator typically uses marginal sampling by default.

The Multicollinearity Problem

Correlated features cause two distinct problems. The first is estimation bias, already flagged in the introduction. Classic estimators “remove” a feature by drawing a replacement from its marginal distribution, independently of the features that stay. When features are correlated, this produces combinations that never occur in real data, such as dozens of transactions in the past hour with almost no money spent, or more transactions in the past hour than in the past day. Every Shapley value computed from them inherits the error.

The second is credit dilution. Even if every \(f_S\) were computed exactly, Shapley values would split a correlated group’s joint contribution among its members. Each member gets only a fraction, so none of them looks important, even though the group as a whole clearly matters. The split itself is fragile: two near-duplicate features, such as transaction counts over the past 30 minutes and the past hour, are interchangeable to the model, so one training run may lean on the first and the next on the second, and the credit moves with it.

These need different fixes. Conditional Shapley values (Aas et al. 2021) fix the first problem but do not fix the second. The groupShapley method (Jullum et al. 2021) fixes the second problem directly and, as a side effect rather than by design, also sidesteps the first, at least for members of the same group.

Conditional Shapley values: fix the estimation bias

Every Shapley estimator needs \(f_S(x_S)\), the model’s prediction when only the features in \(S\) are known. Classic estimators draw the missing features from their marginal distribution, independently of the features that remain:

\[ f_S^{\text{marg}}(x_S) = \int f\big(x_S,\, x_{\bar S}\big)\, p\big(x_{\bar S}\big)\, dx_{\bar S} \]

With correlated features, this produces the same kind of unrealistic combinations described above. The model’s predictions on these inputs are extrapolations, and they feed directly into every Shapley value.

Conditional Shapley values (Aas et al. 2021) draw the missing features from their distribution given the features that remain:

\[ f_S^{\text{cond}}(x_S) = \int f\big(x_S,\, x_{\bar S}\big)\, p\big(x_{\bar S} \mid x_S\big)\, dx_{\bar S} \]

Notation. \(f_S\), \(x_S\) and \(\bar S\) are as in the SHAP section; \(p(x_{\bar S})\) is the marginal and \(p(x_{\bar S} \mid x_S)\) the conditional distribution of the missing features.

Every input the model sees is now one that could occur in real data.

The Shapley formula is unchanged: the values still sum to the prediction minus the average prediction, and equal contributions still get equal credit. What changes is what the values measure. Under marginal sampling, a feature the model never uses always gets zero. Under conditional sampling, switching a feature on also brings in information about its correlated partners, so a proxy the model ignores can take part of the credit of the feature it stands in for. The values describe the model together with the data’s dependence structure, not the model alone.

The price is that the conditional distribution must be estimated, which gets harder as the number of features grows. Common approaches reuse training rows weighted by their similarity to the input, assume a Gaussian or Gaussian-copula dependence structure (Aas et al. 2021), or fit conditional inference trees, which also handle categorical features (Redelmeier et al. 2020). FastSHAP’s surrogate (Jethani et al. 2022) instead learns the conditional prediction directly. Whichever method is used, its errors carry into the Shapley values.

Conditional sampling fixes the inputs, not the credit split: a correlated group’s contribution is still divided among its members. That is the problem groupShapley addresses.

groupShapley: fix the credit dilution and the bias

Conditional sampling still divides a correlated group’s contribution among its members. groupShapley (Jullum et al. 2021) removes the split by applying the Shapley formula to groups of features instead of single features:

\[ \varphi_{G_i}(x) = \sum_{T \subseteq \mathcal{G} \setminus \{G_i\}} \frac{|T|!\,(K - |T| - 1)!}{K!} \big[ f_{T \cup G_i}(x_{T \cup G_i}) - f_T(x_T) \big] \]

Notation. \(\mathcal{G} = \{G_1, \dots, G_K\}\) is a partition of the features into \(K\) groups; \(T\) is a combination of the other groups and \(|T|\) the number of groups in it; \(f_T\) is the model’s prediction when only the features in the groups of \(T\) are known, computed as in the SHAP section.

The usual Shapley properties carry over to groups: group values sum to the prediction minus the average prediction, and equal contributions get equal credit. The cost also drops, from \(2^{|F|-1}\) combinations per value to \(2^{K-1}\): collapsing 50 features into 5 groups cuts the count from about \(5.6 \times 10^{14}\) to 16.

Each group gets one number, so nothing is left to divide among its members (Figure 7). Grouping also fixes the estimation bias: a group’s members are always switched on or off together, so they never appear in combinations that contradict each other. If correlated features share a group, the groups are close to independent, and drawing missing groups from their marginal distribution produces realistic inputs, with no need to model the dependence structure. The price is resolution: groupShapley says how much a group matters, not which member carries the signal.

Groups can be defined by meaning, for example demographic features versus payment history, or by dependence, by clustering correlated features. Groups by meaning are easier to explain; groups by dependence are more likely to keep correlated features together, which is what removes the bias.

The two fixes are complementary: groupShapley resolves credit dilution and, with a good grouping, the bias too; conditional sampling removes the bias without grouping, at the cost of modelling the dependence structure.
